# Checklist for Unexamined Expression Patterns

> **Language / 语言**: **English (current)** · [中文](ai-trace-checklist.zh.md)

> After every revision, run the text through this checklist item by item.
> Goal: surface **any stylistic inertia that slipped into the text without authorial reflection** — whether it comes from AI generation, academic boilerplate, or the unconscious mimicry of heavy theory reading. The core test is not "was this written by AI?" but "did the author consciously choose this way of putting it?"

---

## 1. High-frequency clichés (examine on sight)

The expressions below are common "slips into autopilot" — their source may be AI, or it may be the inertia built up from years of reading academic literature. If they appear in revised text, prompt the author to examine: is this an expression I chose on purpose, or an unconscious default?

| AI cliché | Problem | Alternative strategy |
|---------|------|---------|
| "It is worth noting that" | Hollow lead-in that manufactures false emphasis | Just say the thing; no need to announce it first |
| "It is not difficult to see that" | Presumes the reader agrees, suppressing critique | Delete it; give the finding directly |
| "It is true that … but …" | An over-tidy concession structure | Use a more natural pivot, or split into two sentences |
| "This prompts us to reflect on" | A hollow transition | Say directly what the reflection is |
| "In summary" / "Taken together" | A mechanical summary marker | Use a transition that carries actual content |
| "fills a gap in the literature on XX" | Excessive self-promotion | "offers a new perspective," "reveals an overlooked dimension" |
| "has important theoretical and practical significance" | A hollow value claim | Spell out what the significance is, or delete it |
| "Building on this" | An over-frequent connective | Often it can simply be dropped |
| "This paper aims to" | An overly formal statement of purpose | If the author tends to write "what I want to explore is," keep the author's phrasing |
| "In light of this" | A stiff causal connective | Use a more natural way of expressing the cause |

## 2. AI traces at the sentence level

### Overuse of the passive voice
- ❌ "The concept is widely employed to …" → ✅ "Scholars widely employ this concept to …"
- ❌ "This phenomenon is regarded as …" → ✅ "In my view, this phenomenon is …" (if the author tends to use the first person)

### Overly uniform sentence rhythm
AI-generated text tends toward uniform sentence length and symmetrical structure. Human writing runs long and short, fast and slow. Check:
- Are five or more consecutive sentences of nearly the same length?
- Is every paragraph built on the same "general-then-specific" structure?
- Is the text missing short sentences, sentence fragments, and rhetorical questions — its "breathing points"?

### Over-tidy parallelism
- ❌ "First, …; second, …; third, …" with every item identically structured
- ✅ Parallelism in humanities writing grows organically — uneven in length, allowed to be asymmetrical

### Pile-ups of transition words
- ❌ "Moreover … at the same time … in addition … meanwhile … furthermore …"
- ✅ A good argument doesn't need every sentence welded to the next with a connective; sometimes a clean break is more forceful

## 3. Person and subjectivity check

This is the dimension most easily destroyed by AI.

### Check items:
- [ ] Has the author's original "I argue" been replaced with "this paper argues"?
- [ ] Has the author's original "in my view" been replaced with "it can be held that"?
- [ ] Has the author's original "I will introduce" been replaced with "the following will introduce"?
- [ ] In the revised paragraph, is the author's "voice" still present? Or has it become an anonymous, "objective" narrator?

### Principle:
If the writing-style profile records that the author tends to use the first person, then AI revisions **absolutely must not** remove or replace the first person. This is not "insufficiently academic" — it is this scholar's writing personality.

---

## 4. Logical over-filling

AI tends to spell out every step of the reasoning, leaving no leaps. But in humanities writing, measured leaps and white space are part of the style.

### Check items:
- [ ] Did the revision stuff a transition sentence between every pair of points? (The original may have omitted them on purpose.)
- [ ] Did the revision turn all of the author's implicit premises into explicit statements? (Sometimes the implicit is more forceful than the explicit.)
- [ ] Did the revised paragraph become a strict logical chain of "therefore … so … from which it follows …"? (The author's argument may run more like a spiral than a straight line.)

---

## 5. Terminology and concept level

### Over-explanation
- ❌ Adding a parenthetical gloss every single time a term appears
- ✅ Explain on first appearance; thereafter trust the reader's memory

### Concept flattening
- ❌ Reducing a concept with multiple meanings to a single definition
- ✅ Preserve the concept's tension and polysemy — in humanities writing this is functional

### Over-enforced terminological consistency
- ❌ AI tends to use one and only one word for a given concept throughout
- ✅ In humanities writing, referring to the same concept in different ways (e.g., "globalization" / "this phenomenon" / "this process") actually gives the text more flex

---

## 6. Rapid scan procedure

After every revision, scan quickly in the following order:

1. **Ctrl+F the high-frequency clichés**: search for "worth noting," "not difficult to see," "fills a gap," "in summary"
2. **Read it aloud**: read the revised paragraph out loud — if it reads like "a standard academic paper" rather than "a person thinking hard," the AI traces are too heavy
3. **Compare rhythm against the original**: put the revised paragraph next to the original — are sentence length, rhythm, and tone consistent?
4. **Check the first person**: where the original had "I," is it still there after revision?
5. **Check the breathing points**: does the revised text still have short sentences, rhetorical questions, exclamations — the "breath" of human writing?

---

## 7. Reverse check: the over-imitation guard

Sections 1–6 guard one direction only: AI flavor slipping *in*. The reverse failure mode exists too: a revision that mechanically piles up the author's signature features — first person everywhere, every sentence a comma-flow chain, a rhetorical question at every pivot — until the voice becomes a caricature of itself. The style profile is a portrait, not a rubber stamp.

### Signature density against the base rate

Take the author's real samples recorded in the style profile as the base rate, and compare the revised text against it:

- [ ] First person: if the author's samples average ~1 per paragraph, ~3 per paragraph after revision is an over-imitation warning
- [ ] Comma-flow long sentences: the samples used them selectively — does every paragraph now contain one?
- [ ] Rhetorical questions: has the author's occasional pivot device become a tic?

The test is frequency, not presence. Every signature feature has a natural density in the author's own writing; getting the feature right but doubling its rate is still a voice violation.

### Noise budget: a cap on expression-level changes

- One revision pass gets a budget of **8–12 non-essential expression-level changes per 1,000 characters** (default; the author may reset it). Argument-level changes — fixing a logical gap, correcting a citation, repairing a factual error — do not count against the budget.
- Treat this as a default restraint, not a permission gate. If the author has authorized a comprehensive rewrite or a different budget, continue within that scope and report the extent. Otherwise stop adding optional polish at the budget, finish necessary corrections, and note remaining suggestions; ask only if an unresolved scope choice materially affects completion. This guards against **polish drift** without requiring repeated approval.

### What this checklist is for

This checklist exists to keep the author sounding like the author. It is **not** a tool for evading AI-detection software — laundering AI-generated text to pass AIGC detectors is not a use of this skill.
